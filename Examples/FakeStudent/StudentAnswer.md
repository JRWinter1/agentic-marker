# A Critical Evaluation of Project Management Issues in the Renovation of the Grand Oak Hotel

## Introduction

This essay examines three project management issues encountered during the renovation of the Grand Oak Hotel in Manchester, a 120-room independent hotel undergoing a phased refurbishment programme between 2023 and 2025. The renovation aimed to modernise guest rooms, upgrade the restaurant and bar areas, and improve back-of-house facilities. I was employed as a front-of-house supervisor during the first phase and observed these issues directly. The three issues selected for analysis are: inadequate scope definition during the restaurant conversion, poor stakeholder communication during the room refurbishment, and the absence of formal risk management throughout the project.

## Issue 1: Scope Definition in the Restaurant Conversion

The first phase of the renovation involved converting the hotel's traditional dining room into a modern brasserie concept. The project was initiated by the General Manager, who acted as project sponsor, with a budget of approximately £180,000 and a target completion date of twelve weeks.

According to the PMBOK Guide (PMI, 2021), scope management involves defining and controlling what is and is not included in the project. Burke (2013) identifies scope creep as one of the most common causes of project failure, occurring when additional requirements are added without corresponding adjustments to time or budget. This is precisely what occurred at the Grand Oak.

The original scope specified new furniture, lighting, and a reconfigured bar area. However, during the second week of construction, the General Manager requested that the kitchen pass area be relocated to improve service flow. This change was agreed verbally between the GM and the contractor without a formal change request or impact assessment. Lock (2013) argues that effective change control requires documented processes where proposed changes are evaluated against their impact on cost, schedule, and quality before approval. No such process existed at the Grand Oak.

The kitchen pass relocation added approximately three weeks to the programme and an estimated £35,000 to the budget. A further scope addition, the installation of an outdoor terrace seating area, was requested by the operations director midway through the project. Again, this was handled informally. By completion, the project was five weeks late and approximately £60,000 over budget.

Turner (2014) suggests that projects in the hospitality sector are particularly vulnerable to scope creep because of the operational pressures to maintain service during works and the tendency for senior managers to conflate operational improvements with project deliverables. The Grand Oak experience illustrates this clearly. What began as a defined refurbishment project became an open-ended improvement programme with no formal boundaries.

A Work Breakdown Structure (WBS), as recommended by the PMBOK (PMI, 2021), would have provided a visual decomposition of the project into manageable components, making it far easier to identify when proposed changes fell outside the original scope. The absence of this basic planning tool was a significant contributing factor to the overrun.

## Issue 2: Stakeholder Communication During Room Refurbishment

The second phase involved refurbishing 40 guest rooms across two floors. This required taking floors out of service in sequence while maintaining occupancy on the remaining floors. The project was managed by an external contractor with limited hospitality experience.

Freeman (1984) defines stakeholders as any group or individual who can affect or is affected by the achievement of an organisation's objectives. In this case, stakeholders included hotel guests, front-of-house staff, housekeeping, the revenue management team, the contractor, and the hotel owners. Despite this breadth of stakeholder interest, there was no formal stakeholder register or communication plan in place.

The consequences were significant. Housekeeping staff were not consulted about the phasing plan and discovered on the first morning that they had lost access to their linen storage room, which was on the floor under renovation. This caused a two-hour delay to the morning room service on that day and recurred intermittently for the following three weeks until temporary storage was arranged.

Revenue management was similarly excluded from planning discussions. The contractor's phasing plan took the hotel's two accessible rooms out of service simultaneously, which conflicted with existing bookings and resulted in three complaint letters and a negative review on TripAdvisor referencing building noise and limited facilities.

Bourne (2015) argues that stakeholder relationship maturity is a key determinant of project success, and that organisations with immature stakeholder practices tend to discover stakeholder concerns reactively rather than proactively. The Grand Oak experience is a clear example of reactive stakeholder management. A simple stakeholder mapping exercise using a power-interest grid (Mendelow, 1991) would have identified housekeeping and revenue management as high-interest stakeholders whose operational requirements needed to be built into the phasing plan from the outset.

The APM Body of Knowledge (APM, 2019) recommends that communication plans should specify what information will be shared, with whom, how frequently, and through which channels. None of this was in place. Weekly site meetings occurred between the GM and the contractor, but no minutes were taken and no mechanism existed for operational teams to raise concerns or receive updates.

## Issue 3: Absence of Formal Risk Management

The most significant project management failure across both phases was the complete absence of formal risk management. At no point during the renovation was a risk register created, a risk assessment conducted, or a risk owner appointed.

Hillson (2017) describes risk management as a structured approach to identifying, assessing, and responding to threats and opportunities within a project. The PMBOK (PMI, 2021) identifies risk management as one of its ten knowledge areas, incorporating processes for risk identification, qualitative and quantitative analysis, response planning, and monitoring. None of these processes were applied at the Grand Oak.

The consequences were predictable. During the restaurant conversion, a structural survey revealed asbestos in the ceiling tiles above the dining room. This had not been identified in advance because no pre-construction survey had been commissioned as part of the project planning. The asbestos removal added two weeks and £22,000 to the programme. Had a risk register been maintained, the possibility of hazardous materials in a building constructed in the 1960s would have been identified as a foreseeable risk, and a pre-construction survey would have been a straightforward mitigation measure.

During the room refurbishment, a shortage of the specified bathroom tiles occurred due to a supplier discontinuation. The contractor substituted a visually similar but cheaper tile without consulting the hotel, resulting in a dispute over quality standards and a partial re-tiling of six rooms at the contractor's expense. A probability-impact risk matrix, as described by Hillson (2017), would have flagged supply chain disruption as a medium-likelihood, high-impact risk, prompting the project team to identify alternative suppliers or order materials with sufficient lead time.

The APM Body of Knowledge (APM, 2019) emphasises that risk management should be continuous throughout the project lifecycle, not a one-off exercise at initiation. PRINCE2 (AXELOS, 2017) similarly integrates risk management into its stage-gate review process, requiring that risks are reassessed at each stage boundary before the project is authorised to proceed. Neither approach was adopted at the Grand Oak, and the project team operated in a purely reactive mode throughout.

## Lessons Learned and Recommendations

Reflecting on these three issues, several lessons emerge for future project management at the Grand Oak.

First, a formal scope management process is essential. Any future renovation should begin with a clearly documented project scope statement, supported by a WBS, and all proposed changes should be processed through a documented change control procedure. This would address the scope creep that added five weeks and £60,000 to the restaurant conversion.

Second, stakeholder communication must be planned rather than assumed. A stakeholder register identifying all affected parties, their interests, and their communication requirements should be produced at initiation and maintained throughout the project. This is consistent with the APM's recommendation (APM, 2019) and would have prevented the housekeeping and revenue management failures described above.

Third, a risk register should be established at project initiation and reviewed at regular intervals. As a minimum, risks should be assessed using a probability-impact matrix and assigned to named risk owners. The asbestos discovery and tile supply failure were both foreseeable risks that could have been mitigated with basic risk management discipline.

Finally, the Grand Oak should consider appointing an internal project coordinator for future renovation phases. The reliance on an external contractor with limited hospitality experience to manage stakeholder and operational interfaces was a structural weakness. Meredith et al. (2017) argue that the project manager's role includes managing the interface between the project and the parent organisation, and this function cannot be effectively outsourced.

## Conclusion

The Grand Oak Hotel renovation illustrates how the absence of basic project management disciplines, specifically scope management, stakeholder communication, and risk management, can lead to significant cost and schedule overruns, operational disruption, and reputational damage. The issues identified are not unusual in the hospitality sector, where projects are often initiated informally and managed without reference to established PM frameworks. However, the application of relatively straightforward tools, a WBS, a stakeholder register, and a risk register, would have materially improved outcomes. The challenge for the Grand Oak going forward is to embed these disciplines into its approach to capital projects, recognising that professional project management is not an overhead but an investment in delivering better outcomes for guests, staff, and owners alike.
